Other limits of the data

How the Moon search relates to Mars

Each search starts with the conditions at a site. It then looks for a match on Earth.

The challenge names the Moon and Mars. The Moon search starts with the highest Sun at each lunar region. It then checks Earth areas for these conditions:

  • Hours of low Sun
  • Snow
  • Terrain
  • Cloud

Mars has these conditions:

  • Air
  • Dust
  • A day close to Earth's day

A match there can cover more than the Sun's height. The Mars section explains what its first pass compares and what Earth cannot match.

Who this is for

Teams can plan field tests under a low Sun.
  • Rover and navigation teams. They must have long shadows on real ground for hours, not a lamp in a hall.
  • Camera and vision teams. They must have a real low Sun in the frame to test exposure and glare.
  • Crew trainers and field geologists. They plan walks and drives. They must know the dates and the time of day.
  • Students and teachers. They can open each place and see why summer nights give the low Sun and winter does not.

The page says where and when to look. It is not a site survey. The people who go must check these items:

  • Permits
  • Access
  • The weather on the day
  • Safety

Earlier work

Teams use lamps at night or indoors to rehearse under a low Sun. One rover test used the Arctic summer Sun.
  • In July 2001, Carnegie Mellon's Hyperion rover followed the Sun around Haughton crater on Devon Island. The rover worked with NASA's Haughton-Mars Project (CMU Robotics Institute). It used the 24-hour daylight of the Arctic summer. That is not the same as a Sun that stays inside a lunar band. The Haughton area is on the map and in the short lists.
  • Lee and others (2009) describe the rover tests of the Haughton-Mars Project (paper). It is the only professional source we found that gives polar summer light as a reason to select a site.
  • NASA rehearses south-pole light at night with one lamp. The D-RATS test of 2022 used a portable lamp (NASA test report). The JETT5 moonwalk rehearsal of May 2024 used a spotlight (USGS).
  • Others build the light in a hall or on a prepared field of approximately 77 m² to 1 hectare. Examples are LunaLab in Luxembourg, the LUNA hall of DLR and ESA. Other examples are NASA's IPEx test area at Kennedy Space Center and Astrobotic's field in Mojave.
  • A natural site adds kilometres of real ground under the real Sun. The conditions are not controlled. The weather changes. The Sun moves much faster than on the Moon. The air softens the shadows.
  • Two NASA studies say why this light is difficult. In a virtual drive under a Sun 1.2° high, the worst direction was toward the Sun (Litaker and others, 2024). A review of the risks from this light says that screens cannot copy real glare (Null and others, 2023).
  • The Catalogue of Planetary Analogues (The Open University for ESA, 2012) lists 30 sites. Its "Insolation" field gives hours of sunshine or energy, never the Sun's height.
  • Rover prototypes built for the lunar poles, such as JPL's ERNEST, must use this kind of light to test their cameras.
  • Other teams also calculate the light at the lunar south pole. Some teams show where and when it occurs. We read the available entries and archives. None maps the Sun's height at these lunar regions on Earth with both hills and snow.
